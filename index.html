About

Bio

I am a climate risk and adaptation economist based in Bangkok. I trained as an actuarial economist at the Madras School of Economics, and for more than four years I have worked on regional projects across Asia and the Pacific, mostly with the United Nations ESCAP.

My work starts with hazard data on floods, drought, heat, cyclones and wildfire. It ends with something a ministry can use: a loss estimate, a risk map, a seasonal impact outlook or a training course. I have built a loss estimation method with Lao PDR's agriculture ministry, produced seasonal climate impact outlooks covering 46 countries, and developed climate risk profiles that inform social protection in Indonesia and Sri Lanka.

Before ESCAP I was at the Council on Energy, Environment and Water in New Delhi, where I ran a randomised controlled trial with 4,662 farmers on climate-resilient farming and designed the monitoring framework for India's National Mission on Natural Farming.

I grew up in Kerala near the banks of the Periyar, where floods came every year. That is where my interest in turning risk into preparedness began.

Social protection · Indonesia and Sri Lanka · UN ESCAP, 2026

Climate risk profiles for social protection

The question
Climate and social protection policies are usually designed apart, so the poorest are under-supported after a shock. Where is the risk, who lives there, and what would better protection be worth?
What I did
For ESCAP's Climate Resilient and Inclusive Social Protection (CRISP) initiative, I built the risk dashboard and decision-support tool. It:
  • maps drought, flood, wildfire and heat stress from now to 2100 under different climate scenarios
  • overlays poverty, so provinces facing both high climate stress and high poverty stand out
  • sets out a decade of disaster records and a case study of Cyclone Ditwah's impact and the government's response
  • presents ESCAP's modelling of how resilient social protection affects GDP, employment, inflation and poverty
What it shows
In Sri Lanka under current policies, the share of people facing moderate or worse heat stress rises from 11% in 2030 to 70% in 2050 and 87% in 2100. Provinces with above-average poverty are flagged where that stress falls.
Outcome
The dashboard draws on more than 80,000 data points. Indonesia's profile is now a reference for ESCAP's social protection work in Thailand and Sri Lanka, two of nine CRISP countries.

Loss modelling · Lao PDR · UN ESCAP, 2025

Estimating future agricultural losses in Lao PDR

The question
How much rice could Lao PDR lose to floods and drought in future, and which provinces should resilience spending reach first?
What I did
Co-developed UN ESCAP's method with Lao PDR's Ministry of Agriculture and Environment and Japan's National Institute for Environmental Studies. It follows the hazard, exposure and vulnerability framework and combines:
  • flood and drought hazard maps at 5-, 10- and 25-year return periods
  • rice yield projections from five climate models, under a low and a high emissions scenario
  • damage curves for rice, with a statistical range around each loss estimate
What it found
Under low emissions, expected drought losses stay small, below 5% of rice production. Under high emissions, the northern uplands (Luang Namtha, Oudomxay, Phongsaly) emerge as new hotspots, and losses deepen in the southern rice belt (Savannakhet, Champasak, Attapeu).
Outcome
Presented to 55 stakeholders at a national workshop in Vang Vieng. The ministry endorsed the method, and the workshop's commitments fed into the 10th National Socio-Economic Development Plan (2026–2030).

Forecasting · Asia-Pacific · UN ESCAP and Think Blue Data, 2025–2026

Impact-based forecasting for regional climate outlook forums

The question
A seasonal outlook says where it will be wetter or hotter than normal. What does that mean for the people and sectors in its path?
What I did
Built the workflows behind ESCAP's Rapid IBF tool, which translates probabilistic forecasts into exposure of population, agriculture, energy and infrastructure. I added crop calendars, so an anomaly is read against the growth stage the crop is in. Each season's estimates were checked against observed rainfall, temperature and reported impacts.
What it showed
  • South-East Asia, winter 2025–26. Presented at the 25th ASEAN Climate Outlook Forum. Almost the whole population of the Philippines was likely to see above-normal rainfall, and about 80% of Timor-Leste's maize area was exposed during its growing season.
  • Pacific, November 2025 to January 2026. For island states I assessed root crops such as sweet potato in place of cereals, and solar energy sites. A coral bleaching outlook combined NOAA's forecasts with global reef maps.
Outcome
The tool sits on ESCAP's Risk and Resilience Portal. I presented the analysis at WMO climate outlook forums in South Asia, South-East Asia and North Eurasia. For the 30th North Eurasia forum, our maps of risk to population, agriculture and hydropower for summer 2026 were published in a co-authored journal paper. With SPREP, I produced the first ocean forecast of coral bleaching stress for Pacific island states.
Links
ESCAP Risk and Resilience Portal · North Eurasia forum paper · South Asia training of trainers

Heat risk · Nepal · UN ESCAP, 2025

A heat risk index for Nepal

The question
Air temperature alone understates heat stress. How many dangerously hot days should Nepal expect once humidity is counted, and where?
What I did
Computed a heat index from temperature and relative humidity in Python, downscaled to 1 km, and mapped the projected number of days with a heat index above 35°C under four climate scenarios. I filled gaps in the humidity data by interpolation and checked the results against the World Bank's Climate Change Knowledge Portal.
What it showed
Heat stress concentrates in the southern lowlands along the Indian border, where the index exceeds 35°C for much of the year, while the hills and mountains stay largely below it. The work was part of ESCAP's heat assessments for seven countries and fed the Asia-Pacific Disaster Report 2025.

Flood risk · Hindu Kush Himalaya · UN ESCAP, 2025

Integrated flood risk assessment for the Third Pole

The question
Rivers of the Third Pole cross many borders. Where does flood risk concentrate across the whole region in the coming winter, and how many people live there?
What I did
Built a composite flood risk map for December 2025 to February 2026. It merges terrain, hydrology, land use and the seasonal rainfall forecast into one layer, each weighted by its influence on flooding. The weights draw on expert judgement and published research. I then overlaid population to estimate the share of people in each risk class, country by country.
What it found
  • Risk is highest in south-east Mongolia, western China (Tibet, Sichuan, Gansu, Xinjiang), Pakistan's Punjab and Sindh, parts of northern and north-east India, and Central Asia.
  • Risk is lowest along the southern Himalayan belt: Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand in India, central Nepal and Bhutan.
  • The hotspots reflect strong winter rainfall signals, steep slopes and areas sensitive to cold-season runoff.

Climate exposure · Maldives · UN ESCAP, 2023

Temperature, rainfall and wind exposure in the Maldives

The question
Which parts of the Maldives are most exposed to changes in temperature, rainfall and surface wind, and which climate models can be trusted for South Asia?
What I did
Analysed downscaled climate data in QGIS to calculate exposure to temperature, rainfall and surface wind, and automated the data preparation in R and Python. To choose between climate models, I implemented the Taylor diagram method in R. It scores each model against observations on three measures at once.
Outcome
The analysis supported ESCAP's Maldives Sustainable Development and Disaster Risk Resilience project. I then coordinated and delivered advanced geospatial training for national and sub-national officials, and wrote the beginner's GIS handbook used in it.

Exposure analysis · Cyclones · UN ESCAP, 2023–2026

Cyclone impact assessments: Senyar, Ditwah and Mocha

The question
As a cyclone approaches, who and what lies in its path at each forecast step, and when will the impact peak?
What I did
Applied ESCAP's impact-based forecasting method to tropical cyclones:
  • took six-hourly forecast bulletins and mapped the zones of strong wind (27 knots and above, and 34 knots and above)
  • overlaid population, vulnerable groups, rice production, energy and critical infrastructure at 1 km resolution
  • counted what fell inside each wind zone at every time step to find the peak-impact window, which can set triggers for early action
The method now runs as a QGIS plug-in, so national agencies can repeat it in three steps.
What it found
  • Senyar, November 2025. The first cyclone recorded in the Strait of Malacca in 135 years. Aceh and North Sumatra in Indonesia were hit hardest. At peak impact, 93% of their population was inside the strong-wind zone.
  • Ditwah, November 2025. A rare, rapidly intensifying storm. About 2.3 million people were exposed in Sri Lanka. I checked the estimates district by district against the figures reported by Sri Lanka's Disaster Management Centre.
  • Mocha, 2023. An earlier assessment.
Outcome
Published as case studies in the WMO State of the Climate in Asia 2025 and State of the Climate in the South-West Pacific 2025.
Links
State of the Climate in Asia 2025 · South-West Pacific 2025

Impact evaluation · Andhra Pradesh, India · CEEW, 2023–2025

Does natural farming work? A randomised controlled trial

The question
Is Andhra Pradesh Community-Managed Natural Farming an effective climate adaptation, what makes farmers adopt it, and is it worth scaling?
What I did
Coordinated a randomised controlled trial of 4,662 farmers across nine seasonal surveys, managing survey rollout and the econometric analysis. The programme was assessed on effectiveness, economic viability, maladaptation risk, scalability and resilience outcomes. A companion study of 933 long-term natural farming households in 78 village councils, surveyed from October 2022 to April 2024, asked what drives adoption.
What it found
  • Farmers with sustained support from community resource persons adopted about 50% more natural farming practices.
  • Where women took part in farm decisions, households adopted 14% more practices.
  • Health was the main motive: 84% of farmers cited the harms of synthetic inputs, and 51% wanted chemical-free food for their families.
Outcome
The findings shaped the monitoring and evaluation framework I designed for India's National Mission on Natural Farming, with indicators agreed through 20 expert consultations.

Impact evaluation · Andhra Pradesh, India · CEEW, 2024

Diet quality of women farmers in rural Andhra Pradesh

The question
Does joining a natural farming programme improve what farm households eat, and what actually drives a diverse diet?
What I did
Led a longitudinal study of 2,042 households, interviewing one woman in each with the global diet quality questionnaire after sowing and after harvest. Of these, 438 households were trained in natural farming and 1,604 farmed with chemicals.
What it found
  • Enrolment alone did not improve diets. Crop diversity was low and nearly identical in both groups.
  • Each extra fruit variety in a kitchen garden roughly doubled a woman's odds of an adequately diverse diet.
  • Keeping livestock raised those odds by 29%, and women's empowerment was a further driver.
  • Diets were slightly more diverse after harvest than after sowing.
Outcome
Best presentation award at the 11th International Conference on Agriculture (AGRICO 2024), Bangkok. The study recommends that such programmes promote kitchen gardens, crop diversification and livestock.

Policy roadmap · India · CEEW with GIZ India, 2023–2024

A national roadmap for sustainable soil management

The question
What would it take to manage India's soils sustainably, and how can the practices that already work be scaled?
What I did
As part of CEEW, the knowledge partner, I helped develop the draft National Roadmap for Sustainable Soil Management. Under the banner "Meri Maati – Desh Samriddhi", three regional consultations in New Delhi, Mumbai and Guwahati brought together farmer collectives, academia, civil society, the private sector and government.
What it recommends
  • Treat soil as a critical national asset, and soil security as a national issue
  • Mainstream soil action across government departments
  • Use existing farmer support, such as subsidies, to encourage adoption
  • Create village-level "Soil Stewards", or Maati Rakshaks, and support local enterprise
  • Enable technology suited to India's varied farming contexts
  • Strengthen local governance of soil and bioresources
  • Build a data and evidence system for healthy soil
Outcome
The draft roadmap was launched on World Soil Day at a national convening in New Delhi, co-organised with DAY-NRLM and NDDB Mrida, with representatives of the German Embassy, the Ministry of Rural Development, NITI Aayog, NABARD and farmers from across the country.
